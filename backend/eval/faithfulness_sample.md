## F43-04: What does the bill implementing the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples do?

**Answer**

- **Aligns Canadian law with UNDRIP** – “Passing Bill C‑15, which would ensure consistency between Canadian laws and the principles of the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, or UNDRIP, is an important step forward on the path to reconciliation” [2].

- **Creates a mandatory action plan** – “The bill requires the development of an action plan to implement the objectives of UNDRIP… If passed, the bill would require the Government of Canada to develop an action plan in consultation and co‑operation with first nations, Inuit and Métis” [2][8].

- **Mandates ongoing Indigenous involvement and reporting** – “…requires the ongoing involvement of indigenous peoples at all stages, while mandating annual reports to Parliament” [2].

- **Enshrines key UNDRIP principles** – “Bill C‑15 would enshrine the principles of UNDRIP, which include… the right to participate in decision‑making, with free, prior and informed consent; the right to culture, religious and linguistic identity; the protection of treaties and agreements with first nations; … the protection of the rights of indigenous women, including an obligation for governments to work with indigenous peoples to end violence against indigenous women” [2].

- **Provides a legislative framework to protect and promote Indigenous rights** – “Bill C‑15 seeks to protect and promote indigenous rights, including the rights to equality and non‑discrimination… The bill provides the necessary legislative framework for Canada to implement the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples” [6].

- **Does not diminish existing constitutional protections** – “As also explained in section 2, it would not diminish constitutional protection of the indigenous and treaty rights… The bill would inform the government on how it plans to phase in its legal obligations… provide greater clarity and foster greater certainty over time for indigenous groups and all Canadians” [8].

## L44-03: What does the government's handgun freeze do, and why do critics object?

**Answer**

- **What the freeze does**  
  * The government says the measure “freezes … selling, transferring, buying and importing” handguns, “capping the market” and “will see the number of handguns in Canada go down” so that “there will be no more handguns in circulation in Canada from the moment this bill reaches royal assent” [3][8].  
  * The Liberal government presented it as “a national freeze on handguns … one of the many steps … to get guns off our streets, tackle gun violence and keep Canadians safe” [2].  

- **Why critics object**  
  * Conservative MP Michael Cooper (Conservative) argued that the freeze “will undermine public safety by increasing smuggling and black market activity” and that the focus should be on “targeting the criminals who pull the trigger” [1].  
  * Conservative MP Tracy Gray (Conservative) quoted the Surrey Police Union that “The federal handgun freeze fails to address the real issue: the surge of illegal firearms coming across our borders and ending up in the hands of violent criminals” [7].  
  * Bloc MP Kristina Michaud (Bloc Québécois) questioned why the government chose a “freeze rather than a ban” and noted that the freeze “will not take effect for 30 business days,” asking why a quicker approach was not used [6].  
  * Liberal MP Ruby Sahota (Liberal) said the opposition “are not in favour of … the freeze on handguns” and linked it to their broader opposition to firearm restrictions, implying political resistance [5].  
  * Liberal MP Marco Mendicino (Liberal) blamed the Conservatives for “blocking” the freeze, indicating partisan disagreement over the measure’s progress [4].

## M44-06: Why did opposition parties object to raising the capital gains inclusion rate?

**Answer**

- **Conservative opposition** – The Conservatives have repeatedly “dodged” the question of whether they support a higher capital‑gains inclusion rate and have not said they are “in favour of asking those who are doing the very best to pay a little more” [1].  The Prime Minister notes that the Conservative leader “avoided pronouncing on whether or not he supports our increase to the capital gains inclusion rate” and “continues to oppose every measure we put forward for both affordability and fighting climate change” [3].  In another remark the Prime Minister says the Leader of the Opposition “has dodged that question since the budget came out” and has not pledged support for the increase [4].  The government also characterises the Conservatives as “aligned … with the wealthiest in this country” and “against … the people” [7][8].

- **Bloc Québécois criticism** – Bloc MP Maxime Blanchette‑Joncas points out that the Progressive Conservative government under Brian Mulroney raised the inclusion rate to 75 % in 1990, yet the Conservative Party now says “that is not a good idea” and opposes raising the rate from 50 % to 66 % [2].

- **NDP criticism** – NDP MP Don Davies notes that evidence presented to the finance committee shows “zero evidence that the capital gains inclusion would have any negative effect on job creation,” yet the Conservative member “seems to be opposed to raising the capital gains inclusion rate” and is asked to explain the earlier Conservative support for higher rates [5].

Together, the excerpts show that opposition parties object to raising the capital gains inclusion rate because they claim it is not a good idea, they avoid committing to it, they argue it would burden the wealthiest, they align themselves with wealthy interests, and they are portrayed as lacking understanding of how the inclusion rate works.
